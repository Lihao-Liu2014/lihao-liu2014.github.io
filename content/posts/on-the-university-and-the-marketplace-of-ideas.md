<p>In the past weeks we have read <em>The Marketplace of Ideas</em> by Louis Menand and parts of <em>University: A Reckoning</em> by Lee Bollinger, and so it may be helpful to consider them in dialogue, specifically on the identity of the university.</p>

<p>One of Menand’s central claim is that resistance towards university reform largely comes from the belief among academic professionals that the university does not operate as a marketplace. Bollinger’s somewhat idealistic, normative description of the autonomous university system seemingly suggests that he identifies with what Menand describes. There is, however, more to be said. </p>

<p>Menand’s concept of the marketplace often considers academia’s separation from and connection with other worldly trades. The first meaning is the social or public marketplace, from which the university traditionally differentiates itself. However, he advocates against excessive specialization and closure, which form the monopoly of disciplinarity. This may be the second meaning—the internal marketplace consisting equally of standardized mechanisms of selection and exchange. Menand’s argument is perhaps mainly for a balance between scholarly authority and response to the outside world.</p>

<p>The idea of the marketplace is just as prominent in Bollinger’s book, in that he starts with the Holmesian society of radical free speech where truth ought to be tested through “competition of the market.” The allusion here and elsewhere in the chapter perhaps also parallel the possible workings of the marketplace, the classic spectrum from laissez-faire to state planning. Bollinger’s essential argument is mainly that, to fill in the void left by the absence of ‘state intervention’ due to the First Amendment, the university serves to provide the ‘center’. Partly similar to Menand, Bollinger also argues for a combination of “abnormal openness’ and preservation of the ‘center’. </p>

<p>Therefore, in the structural pictures portrayed by the two authors, not only is the university a marketplace, but it is also a participant and regulator in the grander marketplace of ideas. On the level of ethics or value systems, however, there remain questions. Bollinger’s concept of the ‘center’ that the university helps preserve is specifically maintained by the “scholarly temperament,” values including rationality, openness to new ideas, expertise and “self-correction.” The ‘center’ which used to be maintained by the press was mainly political values that were state-controlled (e.g. McCarthyism). Menand precisely argues that this could also affect the ‘center’ in the university: Political and socioeconomic power, elitism, etc. can reach towards the internal marketplace of academia.</p>
