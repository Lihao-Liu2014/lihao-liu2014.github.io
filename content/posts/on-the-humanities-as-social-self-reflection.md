<p>Readings from the past two weeks have primarily focused on defending and justifying the humanities, exploring their value to contemporary society. One can make the seemingly trivial observation that the texts do so on two progressive levels: first, by considering the humanities as an intellectual enterprise, thereby constructing their values from the epistemic goals, as seen in Drees and During; and second, by discussing it as a part of the general common education. The values of the humanities on the two levels are evidently interconnected, yet not directly identical; here, I attempt to account for their relations.</p>

<p>The most direct relation can perhaps be understood through the model of the ‘three teloi’ as described in my third reflection. In the humanities, the epistemic telos underdetermines the educational telos, allowing the formative telos to take a major role in determining the latter. During’s account of the multiplicity of truths and purposes and the diversity of scholarly practices serve as an explanation for this phenomenon. The result is a need-response model of education, as reflected in the subtitle of Nussbaum’s book: the formative goals, ultimately required by the political ideal, provide the rationale and the guidelines for the student’s education in economics and history, among other examples.</p>

<p>During’s and Drees’s accounts complicate this relationship. Drees notes that the humanities involve higher orders of discourse and are intrinsically reflexive, since common education in the humanities directly influences the public cultures and practices, which are themselves objects of study. Conversely, scholarly inquiry may also critically interrogate the formative telos itself. It is perhaps through this cycle that the reformative forces in common education are enacted. The transformation of human reality gives rise to transformed epistemic content, resulting in transformed critical judgment of the formative goals guiding education.</p>

<p>Pratt’s account of the Stanford debate illustrates this process. The multiplicity of perspectives in the decolonizing social reality transformed inquiry in the humanities, and consequently the formative goal of cultural inheritance is put under critical examination, exposing the problem of representation. Instead of simply expanding the ‘Great Books” list, the newly proposed educational goals as seen in Nussbaum and Pratt involve themes including “critical reasoning,” “imagination,” etc. These are notably intellectual capacities; thus, the transformed formative goals are directly supplied internally from the epistemic process, instead of externally imposed.</p>

<p>This nevertheless raises questions about how the humanities should be justified. The transformed model now relies internally on itself, and thus justification through the need-response system may not be sufficient. However, the answer may possibly be found in the reflexive process itself: humanistic inquiry arises from human self-understandings and in turn critically examines them. The value of the humanities may therefore lie in sustaining this very process through which societies examine their needs and educational ideals and attempt to reconile them with reality.</p>
