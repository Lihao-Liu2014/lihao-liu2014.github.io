<p>The complexity and variety of psychoanalytic readings of Shakespeare’s <em>Hamlet</em> have been compared by Bruce Fink to a “minefield” (92). Whereas Sigmund Freud, in <em>The</em><em> Interpretation of Dreams</em>, addresses the temporality of <em>Oedipus</em><em> the King</em> in a largely speculative manner, Ernest Jones and Jacques Lacan each develop an expanded and systematic approach to time in their respective readings of <em>Hamlet</em>. Through an examination of Lacan’s reading and its comparison to Jones’s, this essay argues that the distinctions in their conceptions of temporality in drama reflect different models of causality and yield distinct implications.</p>

<p>Lacan’s reading imposes his psychoanalytic theory, which I argue is structural and spatial and underlies his perception of theatrical time. The allusion to temporality most central to Lacan’s reading is perhaps the “time of the Other” (17). He makes the distinction between the fantasies of perversion and neurosis; the former is “in space” and “outside of time” (17), while the latter regards the time of the Other. This ostensibly emphasizes the role of time but instead stems from structure: both reach for the upper level beyond demand, yet whereas the pervert forms a stable and “namable” (17) fantasy in relation to <em>l</em><em>’</em><em>objet</em><em> a</em>, the neurotic dwells on the term $, the questioning subject, for he cannot accept the existence of lack in the Other and still keeps on asking “<em>Che </em><em>Vuoi</em>” (13). It is this instability in the spatial structure of desire that leads to the neurotic’s temporal deferral, thus Lacan’s wordplay of the hour (<em>l</em><em>’</em><em>heurre</em>) as a lure (<em>leurre</em>) displaced from the current moment (11). The notion of the “time of the Other” is therefore introduced by Lacan as a tool through which to connect the observation that Hamlet, in Shakespeare’s narration, is “constantly suspended” (17) in other characters’ time to its cause, which is spatial instability of the neurotic fantasy.</p>

<p>Another evidence of Lacan’s stress in spatiality lies in his reinterpretation of “<em>das</em> <em>U</em><em>nheimlich</em><em>e</em>” (22). In the Freudian model of repression, <em>das </em><em>Unheimlich</em><em>e</em> is “that species of the frightening that goes back to what was once well known and had long been familiar” (Freud, 124). The term thus contains an obvious element of temporality; in the class we have also related this to “<em>Nachtr</em><em>ä</em><em>glichkeit</em>”, also pointing at the deferred action where the repressed idea returns. Cynthia Chase extends the notion of <em>das </em><em>Unheimliche</em><em> </em>to the bizarre riddles of the Sphinx and of dreams, which Oedipus and Freud respectively solved with intelligible, “<em>heimlich</em>[<em>e</em>]” narratives (62); these resolutions are intrinsically equipped with a “temporal structure” (63), respectively the stages of human life and the stages of the development of sexuality. Through <em>das </em><em>Unheimliche</em>, Freudian analysis thus shares with the text the common temporality of belated recognition. Conversely, Lacan displaces this notion with topological hermeneutics, describing <em>das </em><em>Unheimliche</em> as related to a set of spatial events: Ophelia’s estrangement to Hamlet is explained by the decomposition of fantasy and the joining of <em>l’objet</em><em> a</em> onto her image (22). Unlike the Freudian interpretation where dynamics underlie mystery, Lacan takes <em>das </em><em>Unheimliche</em> at its face value: outside of home, or a spatial, topological “estrangement” (21). </p>

<p>One might object that Lacan also draws from the temporal arrangement within the play to demonstrate the development of the psyche, an example being the three stages of Hamlet’s relation to Ophelia (22-24). Yet for Lacan this temporality is secondary and emerges from the reconfiguration of spatial structure. Lacanian psychoanalysis with its (post)structuralist formulation inherently prohibits immediate communication; the subject has access only to what its consciousness may perceive or articulate through signifiers. Thus, in approaching <em>Hamlet</em> as an analyst, Lacan reads events and discourses from Hamlet’s most intimate perspective; other characters, Laertes, Ophelia, and Gertrude, through their discourse, act as occupants of structural positions within the “essential co-ordinates of this topology” (11). This scheme resembles the play’s own staging of power within the royal court. The death of Ophelia is understood only through her leaving the position as the object (37); the Ghost’s revelation inaugurates the lack in the Other (Fink, 105). The temporal stages are but necessarily produced by the events on this theater stage of topology. Indeed, the reference to time in “the time of the Other” is precisely time restricted by the structure of the neurotic fantasy.</p>

<p>A critic’s perception of temporality also reflects their model of causality and procedure of psychoanalysis. As discussed in class, Jones, in a Freudian fashion, stresses the temporality in the play as the main mechanism of repression. Jones reads theatrical time at its literal level because his approach, like Freud’s, is inherently etiological. Jones’s investigation enters in pretending “that Hamlet was a living person” (22); to him, the events in the play are the factual content of a case history, which can be used by analysis to “inquire what measure of man such a person must have been to feel and act” (22). Therefore, unlike Lacan’s perspective from Hamlet’s own unconscious, Jones positions the analysis outside the main hero and treats other characters as equally psychological beings with their own projects. On the other hand, Jones distrusts the verbal information of theatrical discourse: Hamlet does not express clearly his mental conflict because he is “unaware of its nature” himself (Jones, 57). Spoken lines become “manifestations” (58) or symptoms for analysis; to break this obstruction, the presence of an etiological analyst is required. Indeed, Jones inserts the Oedipus complex as an interpretive origin underlying the temporal structure of Hamlet’s case history. The matter is most different for Lacan, where theatrical discourse is taken at face value and as explanatory of the unconscious; temporality is seen as caused, again, by displacements in the unconscious’ structure and hence figurative instead of real. Discourse itself relates to the cause, and temporality is the manifested symptom. </p>

<p>Below I list several examples. Hamlet famously said to Ophelia: “Get thee to a nunnery!” (3.1.120) Both critics mention that “nunnery” may signify “brothel” in Elizabethan times (Jones, 97; Lacan, 23). Jones, taking such “misogyny” (98) as symptom, proceeds to trace to its cause in the next paragraphs—on one hand a “sinister” (97) attack knowing that Ophelia “is sent to lure him on and … to betray him” (97), and on the other the repression of Hamlet’s “sexual feelings” (98). By contrast, Lacan unpacks the semantics of the word “brothel” directly in the deep unconscious structure: being associated with “child-bearing” (23), Ophelia comes to occupy the place of “the phallus, exteriorized and rejected” (23). This spatial Discourse is moreover so trusted by Lacan that even seemingly strained interpretations like “Ophelia is <em>O phallos</em>” (20) or “dead men’s fingers … is related to the mandrake and hence to the phallic element” (23) is meaningful to the analysis. Another example that both critics concern is Hamlet’s remarks after killing Polonius: “I took thee for thy better.” (3.4.30) Jones, as perhaps any usual audience does, first interprets “thy better” as Claudius; he then expounds on the underlying psychic process as the disappointment after the momentary “irrational ‘wish-fulfillment’” (37). Lacan, however, takes “thy better” to mean the phallus, in effect the “signifier of power, of potency” (51), in that Hamlet was not after Claudius but something even “better”, an interpretation that Shakespeare is obviously unlikely to have intended. The above clearly shows distinction in analytical procedure.</p>

<p>In sum, by comparing the two treatments, it becomes clear that, whereas Jones reads theatrical temporality as symptomatic with an origin that needs be excavated, Lacan reads temporality as secondary and resulting from structural reconfigurations, revealed by discourse.<br></p>
