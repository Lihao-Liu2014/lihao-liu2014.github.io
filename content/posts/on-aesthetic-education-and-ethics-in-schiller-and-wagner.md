<p>Friedrich Schiller’s <em>Über die ästhetische Erziehung des Menschen</em> was to me an extremely rich theoretical text, encompassing a wide range of fundamental themes, including the relation between reason and emotion, the natural and the moral, the individual and the collective, as well as other sociological and political topics. I will restrict this response to the topic of aesthetic education and ethics, and I would like to refer comparatively to Richard Wagner’s <em>Das Kunstwerk der Zukunft</em>, which I read in another class, despite this comparison perhaps being more complicated than I notice—or conversely too trivial—in a more professional context of German literature.</p>

<p>Richard Wagner’s narrative seems to hold similarities with Schiller, in that both address modern fragmentation, compare it against the Greeks, and argue for a synthesis, or some form of restoration of <em>Totalität </em>on a higher level. Yet, they contrast in their conception of the source and function of art: Wagner argues that art must arise from life itself and from <em>Notwendigkeit</em>, while for Schiller a core quality of art is its independence from the current order.</p>

<p>This discrepancy can be explained through paying attention to the source of normativity or ethics in both authors. For Schiller, it is the ideal of humanity derived from reason in a Kantian manner, independent of aesthetics. Aesthetics, by virtue of its timelessness, acts as a bridge between the subject with fragmented faculties and the enlightened subject; it is functional, formational and procedural, and thus regards education. In Wagner, the normative is specifically the common, or communal necessity, which creates common action and ultimately the artwork which in turn leads to the conscious recognition of this ethics of the common. This distinction is reflective of the philosophies that the two essays ascribed to. Although both refer to history in a somewhat dialectical manner, in Schiller’s transcendental perspective, ethics is supplied externally and thus requires the mediation provided by the autonomous aesthetics; Wagner employs a more immanent, history-dependent perspective in which the current order itself contains the latent force to overcome fragmentation, with aesthetics having a productive and performative function.</p>

<p>Clearly, the topic of aesthetic education is explicit only in Schiller. However, relating to the teloi from my last reflection, one might make the observation as follows. Schiller’s formative telos originates from yet is preliminary to the social telos, which aligns with an educational model as expected. Politically, this may be seen as a somewhat Rousseauian or Republican ideal. On the other hand, Wagner’s teloi overlap: in a sense, it is too an aesthetic education, but formation is now coupled with collective praxis itself.</p>
