<p>In Chapter 3 of Book VIII of <em>Politics</em>, Aristotle regards subjects such as music as “valued for their own sake,” not because they are means to no end, but because they belong to the proper use of leisure, which are ends in themselves. This sheds light on the persistent question of disinterestedness versus utility from the past readings: non-utilitarian education is not necessarily ateleological. Moreover, it seems that no education is ateleological. In the preceding chapter, Aristotle suggests that the public confusion at the question of what should be taught comes from the disagreement over what education should aim at. In other words, the choice of curriculum already presupposes some conceptions of the goals of education. If education is necessarily for an end, how can this be reconciled with the ideal of disinterested learning?</p>

<p>Seneca’s <em>Moral Letters to Lucilius</em> are helpful since he makes an important distinction, one between the internal aim of inquiry in the liberal arts at the time—the investigation of Homer being his notable example—and the formative goal of education for which he advocates, which is virtue. It is also within this perspective of formation that Aristotle’s teleological arguments for education lie. He argues, firstly, to generally educate for the polity of the city (“training for an end which is common”); and secondly, to teach specific subjects like music and poetry for use of leisure. For both of these, he uses the word “character.” It thus seems that the teleological nature of education appears exactly when instruction is understood as formative, i.e. aimed at character development. In both Aristotle and Seneca—and in our readings on <em>Bildung</em>—education is first regarded as the formation of character; then, moral or ethical arguments are made on what an appropriate formation entails; hence the teloi in education. Arguments on benevolent individual formation doubtless connect strongly to broader social ideals, which is for Aristotle the flourishing of the polity and for Seneca a community grounded in stoic virtues.</p>

<p>It is then natural to question how the natural sciences, as we nowadays know them, might fit into this model. As described above, Seneca makes the distinction between epistemic success and the successful formation of a person. One might therefore distinguish the epistemic telos of a discipline, as in knowledge-for-knowledge’s-sake, from the educational telos resulting from the perspective of formation. The answer then is perhaps that, in the natural sciences, the epistemic telos seems to constrain the educational telos more strongly than in the humanities. </p>
