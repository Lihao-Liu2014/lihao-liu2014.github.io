<p>In the first chapter of Menand’s <em>The Marketplace of Ideas</em>, he concludes that “practical ambition” is compatible with “disinterestedness,” and he condenses the two elements of its proof into the fascinating saying: “garbage is garbage, but the <em>history</em> of garbage is scholarship.” The two clauses are juxtaposed, reflecting the argument of free transposition between any liberal art and its corresponding practical skill. Meanwhile they are asymmetrical: the first clause, a tautology, symbolizes the instinctiveness, empiricism, and conventionality in the ways of thinking in practical subjects; the second clause appears more objective and therefore contains a “disinterested” character. I think the saying clearly conveys a hierarchy where the latter is more intellectually ‘respectable’, but that the connotation can be read in two ways. Menand reads the optimistic sense that adding “the history/theory of” prefix easily transforms the practical into the disinterested; one may also read it in the satirical sense which ridicules and rejects this hierarchy and which I am personally more inclined to. </p>

<p>The second “garbage” in the sentence and “scholarship” hold similar positions because they each represent a realm of knowledge, the former practical and the latter disinterested. However, the disinterested scholarship is a ‘ship’, a ‘schaft’, the condition of being a scholar, which is still on the practical level. We may perhaps then place “scholarship” in the slots: “scholarship is scholarship, but the <em>history</em> of scholarship is scholarship.”) This seems to become iterative, resembling the circularity in Menand’s argument at the end of chapter two that the humanities are legitimized through the pursuit of legitimization because arguments of what knowledge is are knowledge <em>per </em><em>se</em>. These circularities demonstrate the unique, perhaps philosophical, position that knowledge holds. I think the point behind them is perhaps a poststructuralist idea (we can clearly see the lack of the “final word” in these circularities), the constructedness of knowledge, which is also revealed in “scholarship is scholarship”. This revelation helps me understand the significance of Menand’s argument that the liberal arts expose “the contingency of present arrangements.” </p>
